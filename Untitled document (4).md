The highest-converting version should not feel like a software tutorial. It should feel like Marketel has already built something valuable for the owner, proven that it works, shown that it will not create operational chaos, and then offered a financially obvious way to turn it on.

The central shift is:

> Current flow: “Here are all the things Marketel can do.”  
> Better flow: “Here is the booking page we built for you, here is how it earns and protects money, and here is the exact risk of turning it on.”

The buyer does not need to master Marketel before subscribing. They need six beliefs:

1. This is genuinely built for my property.  
2. My page is already nearly ready.  
3. Guests will understand how to book.  
4. It will not create double bookings or extra chaos.  
5. It should return more than $199.  
6. I can try it without becoming trapped.

Everything that does not build one of those beliefs should happen after payment.

# **The ideal journey**

I would make the mandatory experience:

1. Landing page  
2. Minimal property setup  
3. Personalized “your page is ready” reveal  
4. One guest-booking demonstration  
5. One operational-protection explanation  
6. One personalized financial justification  
7. Offer and checkout  
8. Post-payment activation checklist

That is the entire conversion journey.

No 10-step product tour.  
No nested three-step tours.  
No five-step Guest App tour.  
No icon-upload lesson.  
No feature-by-feature dashboard education.

The mandatory post-setup portion should take approximately 90–150 seconds if the owner reads everything.

Nielsen Norman Group’s onboarding guidance is very relevant here: obvious features should not be taught, help should appear when it becomes contextually relevant, and users should not be forced to memorize multistep processes during an introductory tour. [Their onboarding guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/onboarding-tutorials/) specifically recommends progressive, dismissible, contextual help rather than front-loading explanations.

# **The psychology behind the sequence**

The order matters because each screen is answering the next natural objection.

| Moment | What the owner is subconsciously asking |
| ----- | ----- |
| Landing | “Is this relevant enough to start?” |
| Setup | “How much work will this require?” |
| Personalized reveal | “Did it actually build something?” |
| Guest demonstration | “Will my guests know how to use it?” |
| Operational protection | “Could this cause double bookings or headaches?” |
| Financial justification | “Is it worth $199 every month?” |
| Checkout | “What exactly happens if I enter my card?” |

Your existing flow answers most of these questions eventually, but not in this clean order. It also introduces several new concepts—Front Desk, Guest App, home-screen installation, alerts, auto-confirmation, card holds, cancellation, availability and revenue—before the owner has decided that the core booking engine is worth buying.

Every additional concept produces another question. More features can paradoxically make the decision feel harder.

Your copy currently says card verification prevents no-shows. That is stronger than the feature appears to support.

# **Stage 5: Explain operational protection**

This is the most important screen after the personalized preview.

The current cancellation example creates the wrong mental model:

That tells the owner Marketel may allow them to disappoint a confirmed guest. It feels like a workaround for overbooking.

Replace the entire booking/alerts/cancellation sequence with one clear timeline.

## **Recommended headline**

> **A guest books. That room is protected immediately.**

Supporting copy:

> While you review the request, Marketel temporarily removes that inventory from online availability. No second online guest can book the same room.

Then show a short visual sequence:

1. **Guest requests the room**  
2. **Room is held online**  
3. **You confirm availability**  
4. **Guest receives confirmation**

Below that:

> **A walk-in already took the room?**  
> Mark the room unavailable before confirming. The temporary hold is released and the guest is notified that the request could not be accepted.

The wording “before confirming” is crucial. It avoids suggesting that the owner casually cancels an already-confirmed reservation.

## **Explain the 20-minute behavior precisely**

If the real system automatically confirms after 20 minutes, say:

> You have 20 minutes to review the request. If you do nothing, it confirms automatically based on the availability shown in Front Desk.

Then the owner must understand:

> **Keep Front Desk availability accurate, and Marketel handles online inventory from there.**

If missing a notification could cause a bad booking, phone push notifications should not be the only alert channel. For a $199 operational product, I would strongly recommend:

* App/push notification  
* SMS backup  
* Email backup  
* Optional automated call when unacknowledged

Then your screen can truthfully say:

> **You won’t have to rely on one notification.**  
> New bookings can reach you by app, text and email.

If only the installed web app receives alerts, requiring installation before purchase exposes a weakness: the buyer realizes the system depends on a browser installation ritual. Build notification redundancy, then move the home-screen installation into post-purchase setup.

You currently use three screens to explain the calendar. Reduce that to one compact section:

Show one date tile opening the `− / +` control.

# **Stage 6: Financial justification**

## **Personalized break-even calculation**

If the owner entered a typical nightly rate of `$159`, show:

> **At your $159 rate, approximately seven directly booked room-nights per month can offset Marketel’s $199 price compared with a 20% OTA commission.**

The exact calculation is:

\[  
$199 \\div 20% \= $995  
\]

At `$159/night`:

\[  
$995 \\div $159 \= 6.26 \\text{ room-nights}  
\]

Round up to seven room-nights.

You can show a simple comparison:

| Monthly direct revenue | 20% OTA equivalent | Marketel | Estimated difference |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| $1,000 | $200 | $199 | $1 |
| $3,000 | $600 | $199 | $401 |

# **Should you charge immediately or use a trial?**

There are three different goals:

1. Getting the most people to enter a card  
2. Getting the most immediate `$199` payments  
3. Getting the most customers who remain paid after 30–60 days

Those are not identical.

## **Highest likelihood of card entry**

If the literal goal is to get the highest percentage to enter card information, the strongest offer is likely:

> **14 days live for free, then $199/month**

The card is required, but today’s total is `$0`.

The purchase screen must clearly say:

> Due today: **$0**  
> First payment: **$199 on \[exact date\]**  
> Cancel before that date and you will not be charged.

CTA:

> **Start my 14-day live trial — $0 today**

This reduces the immediate financial risk while still collecting a card.

I would begin with 14 days rather than 30 because the page is supposed to be ready immediately. A long trial encourages postponement. However, properties with low booking volume may not experience a direct booking in 14 days, so this must be tested.

## **Highest immediate cash and higher intent**

If you want immediate revenue:

> **$199 today with a 30-day money-back guarantee**

CTA:

> **Activate direct bookings — $199**

Under it:

> Try Marketel for 30 days. If it is not right for your property, contact us for a full refund.

That will probably produce fewer card submissions but more committed buyers.

## **What I would actually optimize**

Do not optimize for card submissions alone. Optimize for:

> **Activated, still-paying properties at day 45**

A free trial can make the card-entry metric look excellent while producing cancellations, forgotten subscriptions, disputes and inactive properties.

I would launch the redesigned journey with one offer, then test:

* Variant A: `$199 today`, 30-day guarantee  
* Variant B: `$0 today`, 14-day card-required trial

Measure:

* Card submitted  
* Property went live  
* Booking link shared  
* First booking received  
* Still paid at day 45  
* Refund/cancellation rate

# **Why the Guest App should move after payment**

The Guest App idea is interesting, but before purchase it creates several cognitive problems:

* Is Marketel a booking engine or an app builder?  
* Does every guest have to install something?  
* Why is there no App Store?  
* Must the owner install one app while guests install another?  
* Will notifications fail unless it is installed correctly?  
* Why do I need to upload an icon before accepting bookings?

None of those questions helps close `$199`.

The correct pre-purchase description is one sentence:

> Guests can return to your property from their home screen, with no App Store required.

Then teach installation only when:

* The owner is configuring alerts  
* A guest is viewing the booking page  
* The owner opens Guest App settings

That is progressive disclosure: advanced or secondary functionality appears when it becomes relevant. [Nielsen Norman Group’s progressive-disclosure guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/) specifically recommends deferring advanced or rarely needed features to make systems easier to learn and less error-prone.

# **Copy density**

Your current copy is understandable, but there is too much of it across too many screens. People scan interfaces; they do not reliably read every word. Nielsen Norman Group’s long-standing web-reading research found that concise, scannable and objective writing performed substantially better than promotional or dense writing. [Their concise-writing study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/) is especially applicable here.

Every tour screen should follow:

* One short headline  
* One sentence of explanation  
* One visual proof  
* One primary CTA  
* Optional secondary details

For example:

Bad structure:

> Why put Front Desk on your phone? Bookings can arrive while you’re away. A phone notification gives you time to confirm the room is still free…

Better:

> **Never miss a new booking.**  
> Receive an app alert immediately, with text and email backup.

Then the notification visual proves the rest.

# **Important trust repairs**

Before sending paid traffic, fix these:

1. **Broken image icons**  
   The Marketel and Front Desk icons appear broken in multiple screenshots. Even if that is caused only by localhost, test the exact production flow on iPhone and Android. A broken logo beside a credit-card request is disproportionately damaging.  
2. **Do not call card verification “no-show prevention”**  
   Say precisely what it does.  
3. **Do not present cancellation as double-booking protection**  
   Protection is inventory being held and decremented correctly. Cancellation is an exception.  
4. **Clarify what “live” means**  
   State whether clicking the purchase CTA immediately publishes the page or merely enables the option to publish.  
5. **Explain payment collection clearly**  
   Owners need to understand:  
   * Guest card is verified  
   * Guest is not charged online  
   * Owner collects at check-in  
   * What happens for cancellation/no-show  
   * Whether the owner ever receives or can charge the stored card  
6. **Show support**  
   Near checkout:  
   Questions before going live? Call or text `[number]`.  
   A `$199` B2B purchase benefits from knowing a real person exists, even if most customers never contact you.  
7. **Use a stable professional domain**  
   The transition from landing page to setup, Front Desk and checkout should feel like one company. Different branding or unfamiliar domains can trigger doubt.

# **Testing priority**

Do not A/B test button colors first. Test the largest strategic changes in this order:

1. Short conversion sequence versus the current long tour

# **My final recommendation**

The highest-probability structure is:

> **Build their real page → let them experience one booking → prove the inventory protection → quantify the financial return → offer a transparent, reversible activation → ask for the card.**

Your present design is visually good and individually understandable, but it attempts to create confidence through comprehensiveness. For this buyer, confidence will come from three things instead:

* “Mine is already built.”  
* “This will not mess up my rooms.”  
* “The economics are obviously better than OTA commission.”

If those three beliefs are strong, `$199` does not require an 18-screen explanation. It begins to feel like the cheaper option.
